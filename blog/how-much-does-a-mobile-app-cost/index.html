How much does a mobile app cost? What decides the price

Everyone asks this first, and the honest answer is "it depends". An app can be a handful of screens that show information, or a full product with payments, maps, live chat and an admin dashboard. The price follows the scope. This guide explains what moves the cost, so you can plan a budget and compare quotes fairly.

Why there is no single price

Two apps that look similar on a phone can need very different amounts of work behind the screen. One may only read content from a server. Another may handle logins, payments, notifications, maps and offline use. When someone quotes a price without asking questions, they are guessing, or they are quoting a much smaller app than the one you have in mind.

The sensible way to think about cost is as a set of decisions. Each decision adds or removes work, and the total follows from them.

The main things that decide the cost

Most of the price comes from a short list of choices you make before any code is written.

  • The number and complexity of screens and features.
  • Platforms: Android only, iOS only, or both.
  • Native apps or one cross-platform codebase such as React Native.
  • Whether the app needs a backend, a database and an admin panel.
  • Integrations: payments, maps, SMS or WhatsApp, and third-party services.
  • Design: a clean standard interface or fully custom branding and animation.
  • Extras such as user accounts, push notifications, offline use and several languages.

Native or cross-platform?

Native apps are built separately for Android and iOS. They give the most control over the device and the smoothest feel, but they mean building and maintaining two codebases.

Cross-platform tools such as React Native let one team build for both platforms from a single codebase. For typical business apps, such as booking, ordering, learning, field work or customer portals, this often reduces cost and time without users noticing a difference. Apps that depend heavily on device hardware, advanced graphics or very custom interactions may still be better built natively.

The backend is half the app

Most apps are only the visible part of a larger system. Behind the screens there is usually a server that stores data, an API that the app talks to, and an admin panel your team uses to manage content, users and orders. This work is easy to forget when you compare quotes, but it is a large share of the effort. Ask every developer whether the backend and admin panel are included.

Design and testing are not optional extras

A good interface takes time: working out how people move through the app, designing each screen, and testing it with real users. Testing on many phones and screen sizes, and fixing what breaks, is also real work. Teams that skip these steps deliver something cheaper that frustrates users and costs more to fix later.

What comes after launch

An app is never finished on the day it appears in the store. Phones get new operating system versions, store rules change, bugs appear and users ask for improvements. Plan for hosting, monitoring, bug fixes, updates and support from the start, and ask what a support plan includes instead of treating launch as the end of the budget.

How to start smaller and smarter

The most reliable way to control cost is to build a focused first version. Pick the three or four things your users must be able to do, build those well, put the app in front of real people, and add features based on what they actually use. This reduces the risk of spending months on features nobody wants.

How to get an accurate estimate

Write down who will use the app, the key tasks they need to perform, and any apps you like as examples. A good developer will then ask questions, suggest a first version that is smaller than your full idea, and give you a plan with timelines and a price range you can review before committing.

  • Who are the users and what problem does the app solve for them?
  • Which platforms do they use: Android, iOS or both?
  • Do you need user accounts, payments, maps or notifications?
  • Who will manage the content and data behind the app?
  • What is your budget range and when do you need it live?

Questions

Quick answers

Is a cross-platform app cheaper than two native apps?

Often yes for typical business apps, because one team builds for both platforms from a single codebase. The best choice depends on what your app needs to do.

Do I need an admin panel for my app?

If your team needs to change content, manage users or see orders without a developer, yes. It is usually a separate web dashboard connected to the same backend as the app.

Can I start with a small version of my app?

Yes, and it is usually the best approach. A focused first version gets real feedback sooner and reduces the risk of building features nobody uses.
